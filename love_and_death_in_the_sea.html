3. Reading: Enric Sala's Encounter with the Mediterranean

A Decision of Bravado. The sea has nearly taken my life on a couple of occasions. It was never the ocean's fault; it was mine, for failing to respect its strength. In early summer 2008, I was staying on the Costa Brava in northeast Spain. The bay where I swam each morning was suddenly struck by an easterly gale. The clear water had turned into a churning grey stew of sand and foam. Yet, in a reckless moment of Catalan bravado, I put on my fins and entered the surf.

Trapped in the Current. Fighting against the waves, I swam for twenty minutes before realising the storm had worsened. I decided to call it a day and turn back, but the current was relentless. I attempted to body-surf toward the beach, but each wave pushed me under, grinding my body against the seabed. One wave threw me forward; the undertow pulled me back down. I lost my mask, snorkel, and one fin. I was trapped in the surf zone, unable to make any progress toward dry land.

Letting Go and Surviving. When my strength failed completely, I made a critical decision: I stopped fighting and let the water take me. That choice allowed the incoming surge to carry me forward rather than exhaust me against it. Moments later, I found myself in shallow water, bruised and battered, and managed to crawl onto the sand. The entire ordeal had lasted only minutes, yet it reshaped my understanding of nature.

The Ecologist's Perspective. As a marine ecologist, I know how much humanity receives from the ocean: oxygen, climate regulation, and sustenance. Yet we often forget that the sea does not exist to serve our pride. Some days the water welcomes us; other days it demands distance. Since that summer afternoon, I do not enter the sea when it shows anger, and every calm morning is a privilege.

4. Grammar: Narrative Tenses in Retrospect (Past Simple vs. Past Perfect)

Grammar in Narrative Context: Looking Back at Decisions

When recounting a dangerous experience or historical event, we use two main reference points in the past:

1. Past Simple: Expresses sequential events that happened one after another in the main storyline.
I put on my fins, walked into the surf, and swam out.

2. Past Perfect Simple (had + V3): Clarifies what had already taken place before that moment, explaining reasons and background circumstances.
The clear bay had turned into a grey stew before he decided to swim.
By the time he reached the shore, he had lost his snorkel and mask.
